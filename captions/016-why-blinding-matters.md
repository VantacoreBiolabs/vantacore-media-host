WHY BLINDING MATTERS IN RESEARCH 👁️

Knowing which intervention someone received can influence expectations, behavior, care decisions and how results are evaluated—even when everyone is trying to be objective.

OPEN-LABEL
Participants and researchers know the assignment. This may be necessary in some studies, but expectations and treatment differences can affect results.

PARTICIPANT-BLINDED
Participants do not know their assignment. This can reduce differences in reported experiences or behavior caused by expectations.

ASSESSOR-BLINDED
The people measuring or judging outcomes do not know the assignment. This is especially important when outcomes involve interpretation.

“Double-blind” is not always precise. The phrase may refer to different combinations of participants, clinicians, assessors or analysts. Strong reporting should say exactly who was blinded—and whether blinding was maintained.

Blinding can reduce bias, but it cannot fix weak randomization, missing data, selective reporting, poor outcome choices or a small sample.

Next time you read a study, ask: Who knew what—and when?
